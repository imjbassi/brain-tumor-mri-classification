\begin{abstract}
Reported accuracies on a widely used four-class brain tumor MRI benchmark routinely exceed 98\%, but the released split discards patient identity. We content-matched tumor images to their figshare source and recovered identifiers for 4,586 images from 233 patients. Of 906 tumor test images, 761 were traceable; every one belonged to a patient represented in training. Separately, 216 of 1,311 test images were pixel-identical to training images. Removing duplicates did not reduce accuracy (99.22\% to 99.42\%), whereas five-fold patient-disjoint cross-validation yielded 95.19\% overall and 93.68\% on tumor classes. On the same 761 images, accuracy fell from 99.12\% when the patient appeared in training to 93.60\% when absent, increasing the error rate 7.3-fold. A matched random-fold control attributed 2.73 of the 4.03 percentage-point gap to patient grouping rather than the change in evaluation design; the class without patient identifiers changed negligibly despite relaxed proxy grouping. These results show that the released split measures recognition of additional slices from previously seen patients, not generalization to unseen patients. We release the recovered patient map, fixed folds, predictions, and analysis code.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} data leakage; patient-level splitting; brain tumor MRI; benchmark audit; reproducibility; transfer learning

\section{Introduction}
Deep neural networks are widely evaluated on public medical-image collections because those collections make comparison inexpensive and reproducible. Their apparent precision is meaningful only when the evaluation unit matches the intended use. For two-dimensional slices from a volumetric scan, splitting images independently can place adjacent slices from the same patient in both training and testing. The resulting test set is not independent: it measures recognition of another view of a patient already encountered during training rather than generalization to a new patient. Slice-level leakage has materially inflated performance in brain MRI and other medical-imaging tasks\cite{yagis2021effect,wen2020convolutional}, while broader reviews identify non-independent samples and duplicate images as recurring causes of unreliable machine-learning results\cite{kapoor2023leakage,roberts2021common}.

This study audits a popular four-class brain tumor MRI collection distributed through Mendeley Data and Kaggle\cite{ghaffar2024dataset,nickparvar2021dataset}. The collection contains 7,023 pre-split images labelled glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, or no tumor, and has supported many reported accuracies near 99\%. Its tumor images are attributed partly to the figshare collection of Cheng \textit{et al.}\cite{cheng2015enhanced}, which retains patient identifiers, but the aggregated four-class release does not. Consequently, the released folders appear to prevent a patient-level audit.

We recover the discarded identifiers by crop-normalized, orientation-invariant content matching against figshare. This enables three linked tests. First, we quantify exact duplicate and patient overlap in the released split. Second, we compare the same ResNet-18 pipeline under released, deduplicated, and five-fold patient-disjoint evaluation. Third, we run a matched random-fold control over the identical pooled population to separate the effect of patient grouping from the effect of changing the evaluation design. The contribution is therefore not a new classifier. It is a provenance-assisted audit, a corrected evaluation, and reusable metadata that make patient-disjoint testing possible for this benchmark.

\section{Results}
\subsection{The released split contains the same images and patients on both sides}
The released folders contain 5,712 training and 1,311 test images. Hashing decoded pixels identified 216 test images (16.5\%) that were pixel-identical to a training image; 103 were also byte-identical. Across both folders, the nominal 7,023 files represented 6,597 distinct pixel contents. Exact duplication was strongly class-dependent: no glioma test image was duplicated, compared with 106 meningioma, 105 no-tumor, and five pituitary test images.

The patient audit was more consequential. Crop-normalized matching across all eight dihedral image orientations recovered a figshare identifier for 4,586 of 5,023 tumor images (91.3\%) and all 233 source patients. Of the 906 tumor images in the released test folder, 761 (84\%) were traceable: 287 glioma, 178 meningioma, and 296 pituitary images. Every traceable test image belonged to a patient represented in the training folder. Overall, 206 of 233 patients (88.4\%) appeared in both folders. The claim is limited to the traced population and is weakest in meningioma, for which coverage was 58\%; it does not assume anything about the remaining 145 tumor test images. Table~\ref{tab:audit} summarizes the audit, and Figure~\ref{fig:leakage} shows exact cross-split duplicates.

\subsection{Patient-disjoint evaluation lowers accuracy}
We assigned each recovered patient to exactly one of five folds and trained three aligned random seeds per fold. Unmatched tumor images remained training-only. Because no patient identifiers exist for the no-tumor class, we grouped that class by near-duplicate cluster; the tumor-only result is reported separately as the evaluation with complete patient grouping. Training and validation were also group-disjoint. Every eligible image was tested exactly once per seed, and no fold contained patient or exact-pixel overlap across its training and test boundary.

Pooled patient-disjoint accuracy was 95.19\% across 6,292 images (seed replicates 94.91\%, 95.42\%, and 95.23\%; 95\% patient-clustered bootstrap interval 93.58--96.59\%). On the 4,586 tumor images, accuracy was 93.68\% and macro-F1 was 0.935. Across the three complete seed replicates, meningioma was weakest (mean F1 0.898), followed by glioma (0.943) and pituitary tumor (0.959; Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}a).

The cleanest comparison uses the 761 traceable tumor test images scored under both regimes. Released-split models, which trained on each patient's other slices, achieved 99.12\%; patient-disjoint models achieved 93.60\%. The absolute difference was 5.52 percentage points and the error rate was 7.3 times higher without patient overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:controls}a).

The headline comparison still changes both grouping and evaluation design. We therefore built a five-fold random partition over the same 6,292 images, preserving each patient-fold's class counts, model pipeline, group-disjoint validation, and three seeds. Pixel-identical images were kept together so duplicate leakage could not confound the comparison. Random-fold cross-validation reached 97.92\%, 2.73 points above patient-disjoint cross-validation (95\% patient-clustered bootstrap interval 1.75--3.89; Table~\ref{tab:performance}). On the 4,586 tumor images, the corresponding accuracies were 97.47\% and 93.68\%, a 3.79-point difference (95\% interval 2.42--5.36). Thus, 68\% of the 4.03-point overall gap between released and patient-disjoint evaluation was attributable to the grouping rule and 32\% to the remaining design changes.

The per-class pattern supplies a control specific to patient identity. Random grouping improved all three classes with recovered patient identifiers, most strongly meningioma (+7.33 points). No-tumor accuracy changed by only -0.12 points even though its grouping was also relaxed: near-duplicate clusters were indivisible in the patient arm, whereas the random arm kept only exact-pixel groups together (Figure~\ref{fig:controls}b). It is therefore not an unchanged-grouping null; rather, the negligible change despite relaxed proxy grouping strengthens the contrast with the patient-identified classes. Patient-fold means spanned 5.86 points, compared with 0.70 points for random folds (Figures~\ref{fig:controls}b and~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}b). A random-effects decomposition estimated patient-partition variation at 2.18 points and seed variation at 0.83 points; for random folds, the partition component reached the zero boundary while seed variation remained 0.87 points. The zero is a truncated method-of-moments estimate, not proof of literal zero variance.

\subsection{Removing exact duplicates does not repair the split}
We removed every test image that was pixel-identical to training data and collapsed duplicate groups within each folder. Across ten seeds, accuracy changed from 99.22\% to 99.42\%, an increase of 0.20 points rather than the decrease predicted by a duplicate-memorization account. The 95\% confidence interval for the difference was -0.03 to +0.43 points, and a Welch test gave $p=0.081$ (Table~\ref{tab:dedup}). Simply rescoring the original seed-42 model on the reduced test set produced the same pattern. More importantly, deduplication left all 756 remaining traceable test images and all 206 shared patients represented in training. It therefore removed repeated files without restoring independence.

Duplicated test images were not easier for the released-split models: their pooled error rate was 1.39\%, compared with 0.66\% for non-duplicated images. Because ten models scored the same fixed images, pooled predictions are not independent; paired model-level and image-permutation tests did not reach conventional significance ($p=0.12$ and $p=0.060$, respectively). The direction is nonetheless inconsistent with exact-copy memorization as the explanation for near-ceiling accuracy.

\subsection{Calibration and saliency do not establish clinical reliability}
Temperature scaling changed little on the released split, where expected calibration error (ECE) was already 0.0043, but the patient-disjoint fold was less well calibrated (ECE 0.0119; Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). On that fold, eleven errors exceeded 0.90 confidence before scaling and two after scaling, even though aggregate ECE worsened to 0.0324. This single-fold result shows that a favorable aggregate calibration score on the leaked split does not transfer automatically to unseen patients; it does not establish that temperature scaling solves the problem.

Grad-CAM maps\cite{selvaraju2017grad} from released and patient-disjoint models were generally diffuse at the final convolutional layer's $7\times7$ resolution (Figure~\ref{fig:gradcam}). A patient-disjoint no-tumor example contained a visible text watermark and attention extending toward image corners. Because the examples were selected illustrations rather than a systematic analysis, they neither prove lesion localization nor rule out source shortcuts.

\section{Discussion}
The released split cannot measure generalization to an unseen patient within its traceable tumor population: all 761 identifiable tumor test images come from patients represented in training. The practical consequence is visible in two complementary comparisons. On identical images, withholding the patient increases the error rate 7.3-fold. Under matched cross-validation, changing only the fold-assignment rule reduces accuracy by 2.73 points overall and 3.79 points for tumor classes (Table~\ref{tab:performance}). No-tumor lacks patient identifiers and underwent a different, smaller relaxation from near-duplicate to exact-pixel grouping; its negligible -0.12-point change nevertheless strengthens the interpretation that patient identity, rather than fold size, drives the tumor-class difference.

Exact duplication is a separate, smaller data-quality defect. Removing it does not reduce accuracy and cannot repair the patient overlap. This distinction matters because duplicate audits are easier than provenance recovery and may create false reassurance: a split can contain no identical files yet remain invalid when different slices from one patient cross the boundary. Benchmark curators should preserve subject identifiers or publish immutable subject-grouped folds, and users should group both test and validation partitions. Model selection against patients present in training is contaminated even when final evaluation is patient-disjoint.

The patient-disjoint result is not a clinical-performance estimate. It comes from a single aggregated public collection, uses two-dimensional slices, and does not test transport to a new hospital, scanner, demographic group, or acquisition protocol. The no-tumor class has no recovered subject identity and is grouped only by near-duplicate cluster; residual subject overlap is therefore unknown. We assign the 437 unmatched tumor images to training only, which prevents them from contaminating the evaluated tumor population but narrows that population to the recovered figshare component. Reporting 93.68\% tumor-only accuracy makes this boundary explicit.

Partition variation also limits comparisons between models. Patient-fold means spanned nearly six points and the estimated partition standard deviation was 2.6 times the seed standard deviation. Differences of one or two points between methods evaluated on different single splits are therefore not interpretable without repeated grouped partitions. This concern applies to our earlier single-partition result and to published studies on this benchmark; Supplementary Table S5 documents that correction and the other analyses superseded during this work. It does not imply that their calculations are incorrect on their stated splits. It means those splits answer a different and easier question.

Several further limitations remain. The provenance audit verified the figshare component but not the reported SARTAJ or Br35H components. The content-matching threshold was calibrated on realistically transformed positive controls, yet 8.7\% of tumor images remained unmatched. The classifier ignores three-dimensional context, which may contribute to glioma--meningioma confusion. Calibration and Grad-CAM analyses use one fold and a small number of qualitative examples, respectively. A stronger next study would combine external, institution-disjoint validation; preserved subject and acquisition metadata; volumetric models; systematic shortcut tests with background masking; and uncertainty estimates assessed across all patient folds.

For medical AI, overstated benchmark accuracy can shape research priorities and perceived readiness. On the patient-disjoint tumor evaluation, the model rarely called a tumor healthy (7 of 4,586 images) but assigned the wrong tumor type to 6.2\%, with glioma recall 0.944. These are slice-level counts, not patient-level clinical endpoints. The model should not be used for diagnosis or treatment decisions, and the released artifacts are intended for benchmark auditing and reproducible research only.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Dataset and provenance recovery}
We used the 7,023-image four-class collection from Mendeley Data\cite{ghaffar2024dataset}, also mirrored on Kaggle\cite{nickparvar2021dataset}; its released-folder composition appears in Supplementary Table S1. Images were used under their published folder labels. To recover subject identity, we downloaded the 3,064-slice figshare source\cite{cheng2015enhanced}, rendered each slice according to its accompanying conversion description, isolated the non-background brain bounding box, and resized both source and target images to a common square. We compared ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 embeddings across rotations and reflections. A transformed-positive control that applied cropping, resizing, and JPEG re-encoding to known matches produced a median cosine similarity of 0.9988 and minimum of 0.9884; we accepted matches at cosine $\geq0.98$. The complete mapping, scores, orientations, and three label disagreements are released in \texttt{figshare\_patient\_map.json}. Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table S2 provide additional sensitivity details.

\subsection{Duplicate and redundancy audit}
We computed SHA hashes over raw bytes and over decoded pixels resized to a fixed resolution. Cross-folder pixel hashes identified re-encoded copies that were not byte-identical. For a secondary redundancy analysis, we greedily grouped images with embedding cosine similarity $\geq0.99$ while preventing two cluster representatives from merging. Exact-duplicate and redundancy counts by class appear in Supplementary Table S3. Exact-duplicate counts support the primary contamination claim; near-duplicate clusters were used only as a conservative grouping proxy for the no-tumor class.

\subsection{Model and training}
All experiments used ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18\cite{he2016deep} with its final layer replaced by a four-class linear head. Grayscale images were replicated across three channels, resized to $224\times224$, and normalized by ImageNet means and standard deviations. Training augmentation applied horizontal flips, rotations up to 10 degrees, and brightness and contrast jitter up to 10\%. We optimized cross-entropy with Adam at learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch size 32, no weight decay, and a maximum of 30 epochs. A plateau scheduler halved the learning rate after two stagnant validation epochs; early stopping used five epochs without validation-loss improvement. Patient-fold runs used group-disjoint validation by recovered patient or no-tumor cluster. Software versions and all hyperparameters appear in the repository and Supplementary Table S4.

\subsection{Evaluation designs}
The released condition trained on the 5,712-image training folder and tested on all 1,311 released test images. The deduplicated condition removed test images duplicated in training and collapsed within-folder exact-duplicate groups. The patient-disjoint condition assigned each of 233 recovered patients to exactly one of five test folds using class-stratified longest-processing-time allocation, balancing folds to within two slices per class. Two patients spanning labels were assigned by dominant label. Unmatched tumor images were training-only. No-tumor images were deduplicated and grouped by near-duplicate cluster. We ran seeds 0, 1, and 42 in every fold.

For the matched random control, we partitioned the same 6,292 eligible images into five test folds with per-class sizes identical to the patient folds but ignored patient identity. Exact-pixel groups remained intact. One cached partition (\texttt{rand\_folds.json}) was used for all three seeds, and each image appeared in one test fold in both arms. The comparison therefore changes the assignment rule without changing the pooled evaluation population.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Accuracy and macro-F1 were pooled over each complete five-fold replicate. Patient-level bootstrap intervals resampled the 233 recovered patients with replacement; no-tumor clusters were resampled as grouping units when included. The matched random-versus-patient difference was computed per image and bootstrapped by patient or cluster. Variance components used a one-way random-effects method-of-moments decomposition of the $5\times3$ fold-by-seed accuracy table; negative partition estimates were truncated at zero and described as boundary estimates. Released-versus-deduplicated seed means were compared with Welch's $t$-test and an interval for the mean difference. Duplicate-error analyses treated either model or image as the resampling unit, not each correlated prediction as independent.

Calibration used expected calibration error, negative log-likelihood, and temperature scaling fitted to validation logits following Guo \textit{et al.}\cite{guo2017calibration}. Grad-CAM targeted ResNet-18 layer 4\cite{selvaraju2017grad}. All primary quantities are regenerated from per-image prediction files by the released analysis scripts.
